Tybo, Nevada, burned bright—and fast. Ore discoveries began in 1870, but the camp exploded after 1874. By 1877, nearly a thousand people lived here, with a post office, school, newspaper, Wells Fargo office, and enough saloons to serve a hard-driving mining town. Tybo’s smelters depended on charcoal, produced in massive stone kilns built during the boom. Then the rich ore weakened. By 1880 the mines had nearly played out, and most residents moved on. Across later revivals, the district produced just under ten million dollars in ore by 1944. Today, brick ruins, mine works, and surviving kilns mark the canyon where one of central Nevada’s fiercest boomtowns vanished.
